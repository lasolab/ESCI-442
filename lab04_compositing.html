Background

Every Landsat image in Google Earth Engine is a single moment — one overpass, one cloud geometry, one atmospheric state. In Labs 2 and 3 you worked with single scenes, and that was appropriate: both labs were about reading what a sensor sees at a specific time and place. But most environmental questions are not about a moment. They are about a process. A drought unfolds over months. A forest is cleared across multiple growing seasons. A river delta shifts through years of sediment delivery. Answering those questions requires not one image but a collection of images — and a principled way to compress that collection into something analytically useful.

The Landsat archive holds more than 1.9 million scenes from the Landsat 8 instrument alone. That number is not useful in its raw form; it needs to be filtered, preprocessed, and then statistically summarized. Cardille et al. (Ch. F12) frames this as a three-step workflow: filter the archive to a manageable subset, map a preprocessing function over every image in that subset, and reduce the result to a composite that represents what the scene typically looks like. Each step is not arbitrary software design — each solves a specific scientific problem that arises when you try to turn a pile of imagery into an interpretable result.

Before any pixel value can be used in a statistic, it must mean something in physical units. Landsat Collection 2 Level 2 products store surface reflectance as 16-bit unsigned integers — a compact encoding that must be converted to true reflectance values before any comparison across dates or locations is valid. Campbell Ch. 11.4 describes this conversion as a mandatory radiometric preprocessing step; the scale factor is not optional decoration but a prerequisite for any meaningful calculation. And before averaging across a stack of images, pixels contaminated by clouds must be identified and excluded. A single cloud in fifteen scenes can double the apparent reflectance of the pixel it covers, pulling statistics away from the surface they are supposed to describe. The pixel-by-pixel quality flags bundled with each Landsat acquisition identify cloudy pixels before they can corrupt the result.

By the end of this lab you will have built a complete processing chain from the raw archive to a monthly NDVI time series, applied it to two locations with very different phenological stories — Lisbon, Portugal, and the Skagit Valley in Washington — and then applied the same chain independently to a third location, the Gran Chaco of Paraguay. That last application asks you to interpret a record from 2020, a year when fire and drought produced a signal that stands apart from the seasonal vegetation cycle and connects directly to the deforestation history Hansen et al. (2013) documented from space.

Part 1 — Building and Exploring a Collection

Filtering the archive

The Landsat 8 Collection 2 Level 2 archive contains nearly two million images acquired since the sensor’s launch in 2013. That full archive is the starting point for every analysis — but it is also useless until it is narrowed down to the images that are relevant to a specific question. Script L4.1 makes the narrowing process explicit by printing the size of the collection after each filter is applied. You will watch the count fall in three stages: from millions down to a few hundred (by location), then to a few dozen (by year), then to a working set of about fifteen (by scene-level cloud cover).

The order in which those filters are applied is not arbitrary. Cardille et al. (Ch. F12) explain the efficiency argument: filtering by location first eliminates the overwhelming majority of images before the date filter even runs, because Landsat 8 has imaged most of the Earth’s surface thousands of times but each individual tile has been imaged roughly once every sixteen days. Applying the most selective filter first is the difference between a query that returns in seconds and one that times out. The script demonstrates both orderings — efficient and inefficient — and prints identical final counts for both. That identical count is the confirmation that filter order changes processing speed, not the result.

The scene-level cloud cover threshold you apply in this step does something conceptually different from the pixel-level masking you will do in Part 2. It operates on a single number attached to each scene as metadata — an estimate, made by the USGS ground processing system, of what fraction of the scene is covered by cloud. Filtering on this number removes entire acquisitions from the collection; it says nothing about which individual pixels are cloudy. A scene that passes a 50% threshold may still have the exact location you care about sitting under a cloud. That is why the scene filter and the pixel mask are both necessary and why neither replaces the other.

Visualizing what is in the collection

Script L4.2 builds on the filtered collection from L4.1 and introduces two ways to look at it before reducing. The first is a per-pixel count map — a spatial image showing how many times each geographic location was captured across all the scenes that passed your filters. The count is not uniform; it depends on orbit geometry, the overlap between adjacent Landsat swaths, and how many of the scenes over a given pixel passed the cloud threshold. Locations near the edge of the swath, where adjacent orbits overlap, tend to have higher counts. Densely cloudy regions over the ocean or near coastlines tend to have lower counts.

The second is a time series chart — a plot of one band’s value at a specific point across all the acquisition dates in the collection. This chart is not yet a clean phenological record; it samples raw, unscaled digital numbers that include both the surface and any cloud contamination that slipped past the scene-level filter. The variation in the chart is diagnostic: dates where the value spikes sharply above the surrounding observations correspond to acquisitions where the sample pixel was covered or partially covered by cloud. Campbell Ch. 11.2’s description of the histogram as a tool for diagnosing value distributions applies directly here — the time series gives you the same distributional information but spread across a temporal rather than spatial axis.

Part 2 — Preprocessing Every Image

Why the values must change before you reduce

The pixel values stored in a raw Landsat Collection 2 Level 2 image are integers in the range of approximately 7,000 to 60,000. Those numbers do not represent reflectance; they represent reflectance encoded as integers through a standard affine transform. The true surface reflectance — a dimensionless ratio between zero and one — can only be recovered by applying the official USGS scale factor: multiply by 0.0000275 and subtract 0.2. This is the conversion Campbell Ch. 11.4 describes as radiometric preprocessing. Until it is applied, the pixel values are internally consistent but physically uninterpretable: an average of raw integers is a mathematical result, not a meaningful estimate of surface conditions.

Cloud masking operates on a different band entirely. The Landsat C2 L2 product includes a QA_PIXEL band alongside the spectral bands. Each pixel in QA_PIXEL is a 16-bit integer whose individual bits encode separate quality conditions: whether the pixel is cloud, cloud shadow, dilated cloud, or cirrus. The mask is constructed by reading those bits and zeroing out pixels where any of the relevant flags are set. This is a per-pixel operation — not a scene-level judgment, but an image-by-image, pixel-by-pixel quality screen applied at the full spatial resolution of the data. Pixels that fail the mask become transparent and are excluded from any subsequent statistics.

Mapping a function over the collection

Script L4.3 introduces the map step of the filter-map-reduce workflow. Rather than writing a loop that processes one image at a time, a single preprocessing function — maskAndScale() — is defined and then applied to every image in the collection in one line. The result is a new collection of the same size, where every image has been cloud-masked and radiometrically scaled. The .map() pattern is not just a programming convenience; it is the foundation of Earth Engine’s distributed computing model. Earth Engine parallelizes the map step automatically across its compute infrastructure, so a collection of fifteen images is processed no differently from a collection of ten thousand.

The before-and-after map display in this script lets you toggle between the raw, unscaled collection mosaic and the processed collection. The processed layer will have transparent gaps where clouds have been removed — the Google satellite basemap shows through those gaps. The values visible in the processed layer are in the range of 0.0 to about 0.4 for typical land surfaces, which is the reflectance range Campbell Ch. 11.4 describes for vegetated and urban cover.

Part 3 — Reducing a Collection to a Composite

Three reducers, three assumptions

Once the collection has been preprocessed, it can be reduced — collapsed from a stack of fifteen images into a single composite image where each pixel holds one representative value. Script L4.4 produces three composites using three different reducers, and the differences between them reveal something important about the distribution of values in the stack.

The mean composite assigns equal weight to every observation. In a cloud-free stack, this is a sensible and statistically efficient choice. In a stack where some cloud contamination has survived the mask — thin cirrus, cloud edges, shadow boundaries where the QA flag did not fire — the mean is the most vulnerable. A single anomalously bright cloud-edge pixel in fifteen observations pulls the mean upward without any visible warning in the output.

The median composite picks the middle value when all observations at a pixel are ranked in order. It is robust to outliers by construction: whether the contaminating value is the highest in the stack or the second-highest, it does not affect the median as long as fewer than half the observations are contaminated. For the fifteen-image Lisbon collection, median and mean often agree closely — but where they diverge, the gap is diagnostic of contamination that survived the mask.

The 30th-percentile composite — or whichever percentile you choose in the script’s CONFIGURATION — deliberately picks a value from the lower end of the ranked distribution. The logic here, which Cardille et al. (Ch. F13) explain directly, is asymmetric: cloud contamination always pushes pixel values upward (clouds are bright), never downward. A percentile from the lower portion of the distribution is therefore systematically less likely to include contamination. The tradeoff is that it also picks genuinely lower-reflectance observations — dark atmospheric conditions, shadows, or partially senesced vegetation — over brighter, greener ones.

Reading the histogram

The script’s final section places all three composite distributions side by side in a histogram of the red band (SR_B4) over a 50-kilometer region around the Lisbon study point. The histogram makes the distributional logic visible rather than abstract. Before reading any explanations of what you are supposed to see, examine the histogram and describe what you observe: where does each distribution sit relative to the others, and what is the shape of each curve?

Part 4 — Monthly Aggregation and Vegetation Phenology

From one composite to twelve

A single annual composite collapses an entire year of observations into one image. That is useful for mapping land cover, but it loses the temporal signal — the seasonal rhythm of greening and senescence that distinguishes a wheat field from a conifer forest, or a wet-season tropical forest from a drought-stressed one. Temporal aggregation recovers that signal by dividing the year into windows and reducing each window independently.

Script L4.5 moves from Lisbon to the Skagit Valley in Washington, a broad agricultural basin flanked by the Cascade Range. The script partitions the 2022 calendar year into twelve one-month windows and applies the same preprocessing and median reduction to each window. The result is a collection of twelve monthly composites, each tagged with its acquisition month so that a time-series chart can place them correctly on the time axis.

The NDVI — Normalized Difference Vegetation Index — is computed for each image before the monthly reduction. NDVI measures the contrast between red and near-infrared reflectance; live green vegetation absorbs strongly in the red and reflects strongly in the near-infrared, producing values close to 1. Bare soil, water, and impervious surfaces produce lower values; dense green canopy approaches 0.9. Campbell Ch. 17.3 describes vegetation phenology as one of the primary applications of multi-temporal NDVI data: the annual curve of NDVI at a single point encodes the timing of green-up, peak biomass, and senescence in a form directly readable from the chart.

Interpreting the Skagit Valley chart

The NDVI time series you produce in this script reflects conditions at a single point — a specific pixel in the Skagit Valley lowlands. The shape of that curve is determined by what the pixel overlaps, and in an agricultural valley with cover crops, row crops, fallow fields, and managed wetlands, different pixels produce very different annual rhythms. Before drawing conclusions about regional phenology from a point chart, it is worth examining the NDVI spatial composite for the same months and checking whether the sampled pixel is representative of the surrounding landscape or an outlier.

Not every month will produce a visible data point on the chart. The Pacific Northwest receives substantial cloud cover through fall and winter; some monthly windows may contain no clear-sky observations at the sample point after masking, and those months will appear as gaps. This is not an error — it is an accurate representation of data availability, and it is the same information limitation that Hansen et al. (2013) had to manage when constructing their global forest loss product from Landsat data.

Part 5 — Independent Application

The Gran Chaco, 2020

Script L4-EX1 asks you to apply the complete filter-map-reduce-aggregate pipeline — the same engine you used in Parts 1 through 4 — to a pre-assigned location and year. The study area is in the Gran Chaco of Paraguay, one of the four global deforestation hotspots documented by Hansen et al. (2013) in the paper you read for this lab. The year is 2020.

The Gran Chaco is a dry subtropical forest with a pronounced wet season from November through April and a dry season from May through October. At this location, the landscape is a mosaic of intact forest and converted agricultural land — the geometric pattern of clearing is visible in the satellite basemap and in the NDVI composite you will produce. Hansen et al. used Landsat imagery and the same filter-map-reduce logic underlying these scripts to quantify how rapidly that mosaic was shifting toward agricultural land between 2000 and 2012.

The year 2020 adds a second layer to the analysis. In that year, the Chaco and the adjacent Pantanal wetland experienced one of the most severe droughts and fire seasons on record. The NDVI time series you produce should show two things: the normal seasonal arc driven by the wet-dry cycle, and an anomalous departure in one or more of the late dry-season months. Your task is to describe what you see, identify where the anomaly falls in the seasonal cycle, and connect it to what you know about NDVI and vegetation stress.

Your two remaining design decisions — the scene-level cloud threshold and the peak-growing-season month — are yours to choose and justify. The script’s guidance on both reflects what you have learned about the relationship between scene-level filtering and pixel-level masking, and about the southern-hemisphere timing of peak canopy cover. Read that guidance before choosing your values, and justify your choices in your SOP.
